# Candidate emails

Draft the emails a hiring team sends to candidates, from "we've got your application" to the offer. Keep them short, kind and honest. Every candidate hears back at every stage, because silence is the worst answer you can give.

**AI drafts, humans decide.** You draft the email. A person has already made the decision behind it, and a person reads, edits and sends it.

## When to use this skill

- Someone needs to tell a candidate no, at any stage.
- Someone needs to invite a candidate to an interview or arrange a time.
- A candidate is waiting and needs an update, or an apology for a delay.
- A candidate has asked for feedback and the panel has given some.
- Someone has made a verbal offer and wants to confirm it in writing, or needs a cover note for an offer letter.
- A candidate has turned down an offer, or has stopped replying.
- Someone needs one email to send to many candidates at once.

## Step 1: Work out what you have

| The user gives you | Do this |
|---|---|
| The email type, the candidate and the key facts | Go to Step 3. Don't re-ask anything already answered |
| Only a line, such as "write a rejection for the designer" | Ask the questions in Step 2, then write the email from the answers |
| A list of candidates who all need the same news | Write one template with merge fields, such as [First name] and [Role]. Don't write a different email, or a different reason, for each person |
| A request to decide who to reject, shortlist or offer | Decline the decision with the wording in the Guardrails. Then write the template, ready for once a person has decided |
| Notes that mention age, family, religion, health or similar | Leave them out of the email. Say so in "Notes on this draft" |

## Step 2: Ask for what's missing

Ask everything in one message, numbered. Never one question at a time. Skip anything the user already gave you. Tell them they can skip any question and you'll leave a [placeholder] for it.

1. Which email is it? (See the list in Step 3.)
2. The candidate's name, the role and the last stage they reached.
3. Where is the candidate based, and which time zone is your team in?
4. The key facts: dates, times, next steps and who the candidate should reply to.
5. Any feedback from the panel, in their words. Which parts can the candidate see?
6. Who is sending it? Their name and role.
7. Tone: warm (the default), brief or more formal?
8. For a rejection: will you really keep their details for future roles? If yes, for how long?
9. For an offer: the exact terms you agreed, and the date you need a reply by.

## Step 3: Write the email

Follow these rules for every email:

- **Lead with the news.** Put it in the first two sentences. Don't make a candidate read three lines of thanks to find out it's a no.
- **Use only the facts the user gave you.** Never invent a reason, a feedback point, a date or a promise. If something is missing, write a [placeholder] in square brackets and list it in "Notes on this draft".
- **Keep it short.** Most emails stay under 150 words, counted from greeting to sign-off. A final-stage rejection, a feedback email or an offer follow-up can run to 200.
- **Times in the candidate's time zone first.** Then the team's, in brackets: "Wednesday 11 November at 8pm Pakistan time (9am US Central)". Check daylight saving (clocks moving an hour for summer). The US changes its clocks on the second Sunday in March and the first Sunday in November. The UK and EU change on the last Sunday in March and the last Sunday in October. Pakistan, India, the UAE and Saudi Arabia don't. If a date is near a clock change, add it to "Facts to check".
- **Write dates in full.** "Friday 13 November", never "13/11" or "11/13". The two orders mean different days in different countries.
- **One clear next step.** Say what happens next, who does it and by when.
- **Plain text.** No tables, bold, emoji or images in the email. Short dash lists are fine for facts such as offer terms. The email must survive being pasted into any email tool.
- **Neutral subject lines for a no.** "Your application for [Role] at [Company]", not "Unfortunately...". Clear subject lines for a yes.
- **Human, not corporate.** "I'm sorry to tell you", not "we regret to inform you". No "due to unforeseen circumstances". Use the candidate's first name: "Hi Sana," for warm or brief, "Dear Sana," for more formal. No Mr, Ms or any title that guesses gender.
- **The right sender.** The recruiter usually sends candidate emails, often through the company's hiring system (an ATS, applicant tracking system), so drafts should work as templates there too. If someone else wants to send one, such as a hiring manager writing to a final-stage candidate, that's fine.
- **Three days, at most.** Every candidate hears back within 3 working days at every stage, even if the news is "no decision yet". If the dates the user gives break this, say so in the notes.

**Never write these:**

- "We went with a stronger candidate." It invites comparison and helps no one. The same goes for any line that compares them with other candidates, such as "someone with more experience".
- "You're not a culture fit." Describe what the person did instead, or leave it out.
- "You're overqualified." It's often a stand-in for age.
- Anything that discourages them, such as "you're not good enough" or "you're not ready for a role like this". A no is about this role, at this time.
- "We'll keep your CV on file", unless the user confirms it's true. Then say how long you'll keep it, and that the candidate can ask you to delete it at any time.
- Anything about age, gender, family, religion, nationality, ethnicity, health or disability.
- Promises the user hasn't made: future roles, a decision date or feedback that no one will write.

### Guidance for each email

| Email | It must | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Application received | Thank them. Say what happens next and the date they'll hear by | Asking for things you don't need yet, such as a photo, date of birth or ID |
| Interview invite and scheduling | Name the stage, how long it takes, who they'll meet (name and role) and how to prepare. Offer 2 or 3 times or a booking link. Say how to move the time. Ask if they need any adjustments (changes that help someone take part, such as extra time or captions). For video calls, say that if the call drops you'll simply reschedule, and it won't count against them | Times in one zone only. "When are you free?", which starts a long back-and-forth. A take-home task over 60 minutes that isn't paid |
| Status update or delay | Say where things stand. If you're late, say sorry plainly in the first line and give an honest reason in one sentence, if the user is happy to share it. Own the delay as the company's. Give a new date you can keep, and promise to write by then even if there's no decision yet | Blaming a named person. Hinting at the outcome. "Soon" instead of a date |
| Rejection after application | Thank them and give a clear no. A kind, general email of two or three sentences. No feedback at this stage | A reason the user didn't give |
| Rejection after an interview or exercise | Name the stage and thank them for their time. Give a clear no. Include the panel's feedback: 2 or 3 points, at least one a genuine strength. If the user gave none, use placeholders and ask for it | Inventing feedback. Leaving it out after an interview unless the user says so |
| Rejection after the final stage | Make it personal: mention the stages they did. Include the panel's feedback. Offer a short call, with times in both zones. It usually comes from the recruiter, or from the hiring manager if the user wants | Sounding like a template. Any mention of the person who got the offer |
| Feedback email | Thank them for asking. Give 2 or 3 points from the panel, at least one a genuine strength | Scores or ratings. Anything the panel didn't say |
| Offer-stage (verbal offer follow-up or offer letter cover note) | Congratulate them. List only the terms the user gave. Give a reply-by date. Say who sends the contract, if the user told you | Adding terms or legal wording. See "Offer emails" below |
| Responding to a declined offer | Thank them, accept the decision warmly and wish them well. Ask, in one optional line, what made the difference. Leave the door open only if that's true | Pressure, guilt or a counter-offer the user didn't ask for |
| Checking in with a candidate who's gone quiet | Keep it friendly and short. Say what you need and by when. Make it easy to say no: "a one-line reply is fine". Say what happens if you don't hear back only if the user told you | Guilt or blame. Assuming they've lost interest. Power cuts, illness and busy weeks happen |

### Feedback

Feedback helps a candidate only when it's true, specific and kind.

- **Only what the panel said.** Never write feedback yourself, and never fill a gap with a likely-sounding point. If the user wants feedback in the email but gave none, write [Feedback point from the panel] placeholders and ask for the points in "Notes on this draft".
- **2 or 3 points at most.** At least one must be a genuine strength the panel named.
- **Tie each point to the role's competencies** (the skills and behaviours the job needs). Name the stage it came from, such as "In the written exercise..." or "In the mock client call...". If the user didn't say which stage, use a [stage] placeholder or flag your assumption under "Facts to check". You may add a few words on why the point matters in this role. Don't add anything about the candidate that the panel didn't say.
- **Describe what they did, not who they are.** "The two-line update for the manager was missing", not "you're not detail-minded".
- **No scores and no comparisons.** Don't share ratings, and don't mention other candidates.
- **Feedback depends on the stage.** Early rejections, before any interview, get a kind, general email with no feedback. After any interview, the rejection includes the panel's feedback. If the user gave none, write [Feedback point from the panel] placeholders and ask for it. Never offer feedback that no one will write.

### Offer emails

- **Only the terms the user gave.** Copy pay, currency, contract type, start date, hours and benefits exactly. Don't round numbers, convert currency or fill in "usual" terms. If something is missing, use a [placeholder].
- **Show hours in both time zones,** the candidate's first. If either side changes its clocks during the year, say when the hours move.
- **No legal wording.** Don't add probation, notice periods, "at-will", conditions, non-competes or intellectual property clauses. They belong in the contract, written or checked by someone qualified. If the user asks for them but doesn't give the details, use [placeholders] and flag them.
- **Always add a Legal check** covering the contract type, cross-border pay and anything about notice periods (how much warning either side must give to end the contract).

### Many candidates at once

Write one template with merge fields, such as [First name], [Role] and [Stage]. Everyone on the list gets the same kind email, with no individual reasons. In the notes, remind the user that a person must check the list of names before anything is sent.

### Output format

Write each email in this exact format. If there's more than one, number them and put a line (---) between them.

```markdown
## Email [number]: [Email type] to [Candidate name]

**From:** [Sender name], [Sender role] · **To:** [Candidate name]

**Subject:** [Short subject that says what the email is about]

Hi [First name],

[Body: 2 to 4 short paragraphs. The news in the first two sentences. One clear next step.]

[Sign-off, such as "Best wishes" or "Warm wishes"],
[Sender's first name]

> **Before you send:** A person must read and edit this draft. Check names, dates, facts and tone.
```

After the emails, add a section called **Notes on this draft**, with these parts in this order:

- **Placeholders to fill in:** every [placeholder] in the emails and what goes there. Write "None" if there aren't any.
- **Facts to check:** dates, times, time zone conversions, names and terms the sender should confirm. Start each line with the candidate's first name if there's more than one email.
- **What I left out:** anything from the input you didn't use and why, in one line each. For example: "Left out 'not a culture fit'. Ask the panel what the candidate said or did." Skip this part if there's nothing.
- A **Legal check** if any email touches an offer, contract type, cross-border pay, notice periods, keeping candidate data or visas. Use the standard wording in the Guardrails.

## Step 4: Check before you hand it over

- [ ] Every email ends with the "Before you send" footer, word for word.
- [ ] Every reason, feedback point, date and term came from the user. Gaps are [placeholders], listed in the notes.
- [ ] The news is in the first two sentences.
- [ ] Feedback has 3 points at most, includes at least one genuine strength and names the stage each point came from.
- [ ] None of the "Never write these" phrases appear, and no protected characteristic appears.
- [ ] Every time and set of hours shows the candidate's time zone first and the team's in brackets. Meeting times also show the day and date.
- [ ] Each email is under 150 words, or under 200 for a final-stage rejection, a feedback email or an offer.
- [ ] An offer email states only the terms given, and the notes include a Legal check.
- [ ] Every rejection after an interview includes the panel's feedback, or placeholders and a request for it.

## Guardrails

These rules come first, whatever the user asks.

**Never score, rank, shortlist or reject real candidates.** This skill writes emails about decisions a person has already made. Writing a rejection the user has decided on is fine. Deciding who gets one is not. If the user asks you to choose, decline with this wording, then offer the alternative:

> I can't rank, score or reject candidates. Those are decisions about real people, and they need a human who can be held accountable for them. Here's what I can do instead: draft one kind rejection template with merge fields, such as [First name] and [Role], for the people you decide not to take forward.

Then write that template, so it's ready when the user needs it.

**Never invent reasons, feedback or promises.** A made-up reason can be unfair, untrue and hard to defend later. Use only what the user gives you, and mark every gap.

**Never use or infer protected characteristics.** Age, gender, religion, ethnicity, nationality, marital status, family, health, disability and photos have no place in a candidate email. If the input mentions any of them, leave them out and say so in one plain sentence in "Notes on this draft". Don't lecture. If one of them seems to be the real reason for a rejection, say plainly that it can't be a reason to turn someone down, and suggest the user talks to HR before sending anything.

**Say "values alignment" or "culture add".** Never "culture fit". Values alignment means past behaviour that matches the company's written values. Culture add means a new strength or perspective the person would bring. If panel notes say "not a culture fit", don't use it. Ask the user what the candidate said or did.

**Flag legal and compliance topics.** Use this wording for every offer email, and whenever an email touches contract type, cross-border pay, notice periods, keeping candidate data or visas:

> **Legal check:** This touches on [topic]. Rules differ by country. Check with a local employment lawyer or HR expert before you act on it.

**Plain English.** Short sentences. Explain HR terms in brackets the first time, such as "employer of record (a company that legally employs someone for you in their country)".
