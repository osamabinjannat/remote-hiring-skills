## How to use this scorecard
- Rate alone, before the debrief (the meeting where the panel shares evidence). Don't read anyone else's notes first.
- Rate the evidence (what they said or did), not your impression of them.
- No evidence for a competency? Pick "No evidence" rather than guessing.

## Rating scale
| Rating | What the evidence shows |
|---|---|
| 4 Clearly above the bar | Several specific examples. They already do more than the role needs on day one |
| 3 Meets the bar | Specific examples that show they can do what the role needs on day one |
| 2 Some concern | Some evidence, but it's vague, general or falls short in places |
| 1 Clear concern | Specific examples that show they can't yet do what the role needs |
| No evidence | Your stage didn't cover it, or time ran out. It's not a mark against them |

There's no middle rating, on purpose. Pick the side the evidence points to.

## 1. Client communication (25%)
**What it means:** Leads a client call in fluent spoken English and explains software simply to people who aren't technical.
**Tested in:** Mock client call, by Daniel Okafor. Hiring manager interview, by Rachel Moore.

| Strong looks like | Weak looks like |
|---|---|
| Asks what the clinic is trying to do before explaining anything | Starts explaining features before finding out what the clinic needs |
| Explains in plain words and checks the client has understood, for example by asking them to try it | Uses product or technical terms without explaining them, and doesn't check understanding |
| Ends the call with clear next steps: who does what, by when | Ends the call with no agreed next step |

**Questions**
1. *(Role play, Daniel plays the practice manager of a clinic that has just signed up)* "Our front desk team has only ever used a paper diary. How do I get them using online booking without a fuss?"
   - Follow-up, still in role: "Sorry, you lost me there. Can you say that more simply?"
2. *(Behavioural, asked by Rachel)* "Tell me about a time you had to explain something technical to someone who found it confusing. What did you do?"
   - Follow-up: "How did you know they'd understood?"
3. *(Situational, remote, asked by Rachel)* "Most clinic owners will only ever meet you on video or by email. How would you build trust with a client you never meet in person?"
   - Follow-up: "Tell me about a client relationship you built that way. What made it work?"

**Listen for:** Real examples, with what they said and what the client did next. Plain words, and checking for understanding. Judge whether a clinic owner would understand them, not their accent or how polished they sound.

## 2. Handling difficult conversations (20%)
**What it means:** Stays calm with an upset client, owns the problem and agrees an honest next step, even when they can't fix it on the call.
**Tested in:** Mock client call, by Daniel Okafor.

| Strong looks like | Weak looks like |
|---|---|
| Lets the client finish, then repeats the problem back before offering a fix | Interrupts, or jumps to a fix before they understand the problem |
| Says what they'll do and by when, and only promises what they control | Promises a fix date or a refund they can't guarantee, to end the call |
| Keeps a polite tone and stays on the problem when the client interrupts or raises their voice | Argues, or blames another team, the product or the client |

**Questions**
1. *(Role play, Daniel plays an angry clinic owner)* "Our online bookings stopped working this morning. Patients have been ringing the front desk all day. This is the second time this month. Why am I paying for this?"
   - Follow-up, still in role: "I want this month refunded. Can you do that right now?"
2. *(Behavioural, after the role play)* "Tell me about a time a client was angry with you or your company. What did you say, and what happened next?"
   - Follow-up: "Looking back, what would you do differently?"
3. *(Situational)* "A clinic owner tells you they're leaving at renewal because of last month's bugs. You have 15 minutes with them. How do you use it?"
   - Follow-up: "What would you write to Rachel after the call?"

**Listen for:** They name what the problem costs the clinic before explaining anything. Honest timelines, even when the news is bad. In the role play, rate what they did, not how nervous they seemed. Role plays feel awkward for most people.

## 3. Written async communication (20%)
**What it means:** Writes emails, ticket replies and updates that someone in another time zone can act on later, without a call (async means not at the same time).
**Tested in:** Written exercise (45 minutes, at home), set by Rachel Moore and reviewed by Daniel Okafor.

| Strong looks like | Weak looks like |
|---|---|
| Puts the answer or the next step first, then the detail | Buries the main point or the next step at the end |
| Writes short, friendly, plain sentences a busy clinic owner can follow | Uses jargon, or replies in a curt or defensive way, such as "As I said before..." |
| Says what's done, what's still open and who needs to do what, so the reader doesn't have to ask | Leaves the reader guessing what happens next or who owns it |

**Questions**
1. *(Written task)* "A clinic owner emails you: 'Two patients turned up for the same 10am slot today. That's the second time this week. What is going on?' Write your reply."
   - Follow-up, in the same task: "The clinic owner replies: 'That doesn't help. I need to know it won't happen tomorrow.' Write your second reply."
2. *(Written task)* "It's the end of your day and Rachel is asleep in Austin. Write her a two-line update on this clinic."
   - Follow-up, in the same task: "Then list anything you'd need to know before you reply to the clinic again, and who you'd ask."

**Look for:** Could Rachel act on the update at 9am Austin time without asking a question first? A clear order, a friendly tone and sensible questions where the task leaves gaps. Small typos matter less than whether the reader knows what happens next.

## 4. Self-management and account organisation (20%)
**What it means:** Keeps track of many client accounts at once, plans their own work and flags problems early, without reminders.
**Tested in:** Hiring manager interview, by Rachel Moore.

| Strong looks like | Weak looks like |
|---|---|
| Describes a real system they used to track accounts, follow-ups and renewal dates, and how they kept it up to date | Can't describe how they tracked their work beyond "I keep it in my head" |
| Gives an example of spotting a problem early and acting before the client complained | Their examples are of problems someone else spotted first |
| Explains how they chose what to do first on a busy day, with an example | Describes waiting for someone else to set their priorities |

**Questions**
1. *(Behavioural)* "Tell me about a time you looked after a lot of clients or tasks at once. How did you keep track of them?"
   - Follow-up: "What happened when something slipped through?"
2. *(Behavioural)* "Tell me about a time you noticed a problem with a client before they told you. What did you do?"
   - Follow-up: "Who did you tell, and when?"
3. *(Situational, remote)* "What's your plan if your power or internet goes down in the middle of a client call?"
   - Follow-up: "Has that happened to you before? What did you do, and who did you tell?"

**Listen for:** A named system and real examples. The tool doesn't matter, and HubSpot can be learned. For the power or internet question, judge the plan, not the setup. Listen for a backup, such as a UPS (a backup battery), a mobile hotspot or a co-working space, and a habit of telling people early.

## 5. Values alignment and culture add (15%)
**What it means:** Values alignment is past behaviour that matches Fernhill's written values. Culture add is a new strength or perspective they'd bring to the team.
**Tested in:** Values conversation, by Priya Nair.

Fernhill's written values:
- **Clinics first:** put the clinic's problem before our convenience.
- **Write it down:** decisions and updates in writing so the other time zone can act.
- **Own the outcome:** finish what you start, flag problems early.

| Strong looks like | Weak looks like |
|---|---|
| Gives a real example for each value they talk about: what they did and what happened | Talks about values in general ("I'm very client-focused") with no example |
| Names a value they find hard, with an example of what they did about it | Says what they would do, but never what they have done |
| Names a strength or perspective the team doesn't have yet, with an example of using it | Can't name anything they'd add, even after the follow-up |

**Questions**
1. *(Behavioural)* "Our values are Clinics first, Write it down and Own the outcome. Pick the one that sounds most like how you already work, and tell me about a time you showed it."
   - Follow-up: "Now pick the one that would be hardest for you. Tell me about a time it was hard."
2. *(Behavioural, Clinics first)* "Tell me about a time you put a client's problem ahead of your own convenience."
   - Follow-up: "What did it cost you, and would you do it again?"
3. *(Behavioural, culture add)* "Tell me about something you brought to a past team that it didn't have before. It could be a skill, an experience or a way of working."
   - Follow-up: "Our team is four people in Austin and two in Lahore. What might that look like here?"

**Listen for:** Examples that match the three values above. Ignore shared hobbies, a similar background or whether they seem like someone you'd get on with. A different way of working can be a culture add, not a concern. Some people find it hard to talk about their strengths, so ask the follow-up before you pick "No evidence".
